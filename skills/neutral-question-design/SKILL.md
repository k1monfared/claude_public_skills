# Neutral Question Design

Yes/no questions invite the answer the asker seems to want, or the one that ends the exchange fastest. Any hint of your preference in wording, order, or tone makes it worse. The same is true of questions about a habit or a disposition ("do people share work in progress?") rather than a specific event. Write questions that can't be answered by reflex.

## Rules

1. **Offer the real options, not a confirmation.** Replace "Is it X?" with "Is it X or Y?"
2. **The option set can itself lead.** Choosing the pair, or a "vs" framing, can signal your preferred axis or build a false dichotomy ("exploratory or repetitive"). Offer options only when they are genuinely symmetric and exhaustive. Otherwise ask open and let the person name the categories.
3. **Cover every plausible option,** including "other" and "not sure".
4. **Hide your preference.** Keep wording, order, and tone flat, and don't give reasons that reveal what you hope to hear. Your values leak through the topic too: "where do user benefit and revenue pull apart" announces that you care about user benefit. If the topic itself signals a preference, ask a symmetric version or read it from behavior instead. A short piece of neutral context can make a question answerable, but context that justifies your preferred answer is spin.
5. **Balance or randomize option order.** First and last options get picked most.
6. **Make "I don't know" cheap, and don't pressure.** Add an explicit exit so people don't guess, and treat "not sure" as a fine answer. Pushing for the answer you want gets you a fabricated one.
7. **Don't presuppose.** "What would you change?" assumes a change is needed; "What problems did you hit?" assumes problems. Name "nothing" as an option and ask for details only after the person picks.
8. **One question at a time.**
9. **Ask for the instance, not the disposition.** "Do people share work in progress?" or "Are you satisfied with the process?" asks for a self-report and invites the expected answer. Ask for a specific past event: "give me a recent example of...", "tell me about the last time...". A dated instance is hard to fake, and it reveals the disposition on its own.
10. **Don't make the respondent classify.** Asking someone to sort their experience into your categories ("how much was interesting versus repetitive?") hands them your framing and a judgment you should make yourself. Ask for the raw facts and classify them yourself.

## Examples

| Leading | Neutral |
|---|---|
| "Is the sauce sugar based?" | "Is the sauce sugar based or vinegar based?" |
| "Is the deploy ready?" | "Is the deploy ready, blocked, or still in progress?" |
| "You want bullets, right?" | "Would you like bullets, a paragraph, or something else?" |
| "Did you try restarting it?" | "Which have you tried: restart, reinstall, neither, or something else?" |
| "Are you happy with the results?" / "What would you change?" | "Do the results work as they are, or do they need changes? If changes, which?" |
| "Do people share work in progress?" | "Give me a recent example of how work in progress gets shared." |
| "How much of your work is interesting vs grunge?" | "What did you work on over the last five days?" |
| "Where do user benefit and revenue pull apart?" | "Walk me through the last time two goals pulled in different directions. How was it resolved?" |

## Process

1. Write down the answer you're hoping for.
2. List all plausible answers, and note any judgment you are tempted to ask the person to make. Plan to make that judgment yourself from what they describe.
3. Rephrase as a question over that set, with an "other / unsure" option, or as a request for a specific past instance.
4. Reread: could the person infer your preference from the wording, the option set, the order, or the topic? If yes, reword.
5. For high-stakes facts, verify a second way: different phrasing or an independent source.

## Interpreting replies

- A quick, reflexive "yes" to a yes/no question is weak evidence. Re-ask as either/or.
- A specific story with names, dates, or numbers is strong evidence. A generic answer to a question that asked for an instance is weak evidence even if it is confident.
- "Let me check" or "not sure" is a good outcome. Wait for the real answer.
- If two phrasings give different answers, treat the answer as unreliable and keep probing.

## Exceptions

- Confirming an action ("Delete these 3 files?"): state exactly what will happen and make both choices equally easy.
- Small talk, or cases where the other party has no reason to please you and the question is unambiguous.

## Checklist

- [ ] Could a reflexive "yes" be wrong?
- [ ] All plausible options listed, plus "other / unsure"?
- [ ] My preferred answer invisible in wording, order, and topic?
- [ ] Nothing presupposed (problem, change, preference)?
- [ ] Asking for an instance, not a disposition or self-report?
- [ ] Not making the respondent do my classifying or judging?
- [ ] Is "not sure" a cheap, unpressured answer?
- [ ] Single question?
